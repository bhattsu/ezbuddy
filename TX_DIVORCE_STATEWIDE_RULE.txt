FILING ELIGIBILITY AND RESIDENCY

## Can I file for divorce in Texas?

A Texas divorce can generally be filed when either spouse has been domiciled in Texas for the preceding six months and has lived in the filing county for the preceding 90 days. The spouse satisfying these requirements can be either the petitioner or the respondent.

A temporary absence from Texas does not necessarily end Texas domicile. Special rules may apply to military members, spouses accompanying military members, and people performing certain government service.

Before filing, confirm both spouses' Texas domicile and county residence dates. A mailing address alone does not prove legal domicile or county residence.

VENUE AND COURT SELECTION

## Which Texas county should receive the divorce case?

The case is ordinarily filed in a county where either spouse satisfies the 90-day county residence requirement, provided that either spouse also satisfies the six-month Texas domicile requirement.

Selecting the correct county does not necessarily identify the correct clerk or court. Texas counties differ in how divorce cases are filed and assigned. The filing party should verify whether the district clerk or another authorized clerk receives divorce petitions and which courts handle family cases.

A petition submitted to the wrong clerk, court, or electronic filing location may be returned or delayed.

GROUNDS FOR DIVORCE

## What legal ground can be used for a Texas divorce?

Insupportability is the Texas no-fault divorce ground. It applies when conflict or discord has destroyed the legitimate purposes of the marriage and there is no reasonable expectation that the spouses will reconcile.

Texas also recognizes fault grounds, including cruelty, adultery, a qualifying felony conviction, abandonment, living apart, and confinement in a mental hospital under specified conditions.

A petition may state the applicable statutory ground without describing all supporting evidence. A spouse alleging fault should nevertheless be prepared to prove it. Proven fault may affect property division or other requested relief.

WAITING PERIOD

## Is a Texas divorce automatically final 60 days after filing?

No. The 60-day period is generally the earliest time when a court may grant the divorce. It does not automatically end the marriage.

The case must also satisfy service or waiver requirements, response or default deadlines, evidence requirements, child-related requirements, property and debt division, final-decree preparation, and the assigned court's hearing or submission procedure.

The court must render the divorce. The spouses are not divorced merely because 60 days have passed.

## When can the 60-day waiting period be waived?

A family-violence exception may allow the court to grant a divorce before the ordinary waiting period expires.

The exception may apply when the respondent has been finally convicted of, or received deferred adjudication for, an offense involving family violence against the petitioner or a member of the petitioner's household. It may also apply when the petitioner has a qualifying active protective order or emergency protective order based on a family-violence finding.

The relevant conviction, deferred-adjudication record, or protective order should be verified before the exception is used. An unsupported allegation does not by itself establish the exception.

WAIVER OF SERVICE

## Can my spouse sign a waiver instead of being formally served?

Yes. A respondent may sign a waiver of service after the divorce petition has been filed.

The waiver should acknowledge that the respondent received a copy of the filed petition and should contain the respondent's mailing address. It generally must be sworn before a notary who is not an attorney in the divorce. The notarization requirement does not apply in the same way to an incarcerated respondent. A digitized signature may be permitted.

A waiver of service does not automatically mean that the respondent agrees to the requested divorce terms or final decree. Agreement should be documented separately in a complete agreed decree or another valid written agreement.

STATEWIDE PETITION AND FILING REQUIREMENTS

## What should a Texas divorce petition address?

The petition should identify the spouses, state the legal ground for divorce, explain the facts supporting Texas residency and county venue, and identify the relief requested.

Depending on the case, the petition may also need to address:

- Children of the marriage
- Pregnancy and parentage
- Existing custody or support orders
- Real and personal property
- Community and separate property claims
- Debts and liabilities
- Protective orders or family violence
- Temporary orders
- Restoration of a prior name
- Attorney's fees and court costs
- Other relief needed to complete the divorce

Sensitive personal and financial information should not be placed in publicly accessible filings unless legally required and properly protected.

Attorneys generally must electronically file civil and family matters in Texas district and county courts. Self-represented parties may be permitted to e-file or use another filing method accepted by the receiving clerk.

Local rules may require standing-order notices, local forms, court-assignment documents, or proposed-order procedures. A local instruction should be applied only to the counties, courts, and case types it actually governs.

PREGNANCY AND PARENTAGE

## Can a Texas divorce be finalized while a spouse is pregnant?

Pregnancy should be disclosed, and the parentage of the unborn child must be evaluated. The passage of the ordinary waiting period does not make the case ready for finalization if child and parentage issues remain unresolved.

The court must be able to enter legally sufficient orders concerning children of the marriage. Courts commonly delay finalization until after birth when parentage and the required child orders cannot yet be determined.

The correct result depends on the facts, including statutory parentage presumptions, the identity of the biological parents, existing acknowledgments or orders, and the assigned court's procedure.

Before requesting finalization, identify the pregnancy, expected birth date, parentage facts, and any competing parentage claim.

CHILDREN IN A DIVORCE

## What statewide requirements apply when children are involved?

A divorce involving children generally also includes decisions affecting the parent-child relationship. The court may need to address:

- Child-custody jurisdiction
- Conservatorship
- Parental rights and duties
- The child's primary residence
- Any geographic restriction
- Possession and access
- Child support
- Medical support
- Dental support
- Health-insurance responsibilities
- Uninsured health expenses
- Existing custody or support orders
- Parentage
- Other provisions required for the child's welfare

Existing orders can affect which court has authority over the children. If a child has lived in another state or another court has issued a custody order, the court must evaluate home-state and continuing-jurisdiction rules.

Collect each child's residence history, existing orders, pending case information, parentage facts, and proposed parenting and support terms before preparing the final decree.

PROPERTY AND FINANCIAL RULES

## Does Texas automatically divide marital property equally?

No. Texas courts divide the marital estate in a manner the court considers just and right. The law does not require an automatic equal division in every case.

Property possessed by either spouse during the marriage or when the divorce occurs is generally presumed to be community property. A spouse claiming that an asset is separate property must prove the separate character with clear and convincing evidence.

The final decree should identify and allocate assets and liabilities precisely. Important categories include:

- The marital residence and other real estate
- Vehicles
- Bank and investment accounts
- Household property
- Business interests
- Retirement accounts and pensions
- Tax liabilities and refunds
- Credit cards and loans
- Separate-property claims
- Reimbursement claims
- Other known property and debts

A divorce decree generally binds the spouses but does not automatically release either spouse from obligations owed to a creditor. Retirement benefits may require a separate domestic-relations order acceptable to the retirement plan.

FINALIZATION

## What must happen before a Texas divorce can be finalized?

Before finalization, the case should be reviewed for all of the following:

- The 60-day waiting period has expired or a documented exception applies.
- The respondent was validly served, signed a valid post-filing waiver, or otherwise appeared.
- The respondent's answer deadline has been calculated correctly.
- Any answer or other appearance has been considered.
- A requested default satisfies service, return, timing, military-status, evidence, and notice requirements.
- The court has sufficient evidence supporting the divorce and requested orders.
- Child jurisdiction, parentage, conservatorship, possession, and support issues are resolved.
- Property and debts are completely and accurately divided.
- Required financial disclosures have been completed or lawfully modified.
- The final decree accurately states the requested or agreed terms.
- The required parties have signed the decree when the case is represented as agreed.
- The assigned court's prove-up, hearing, affidavit, submission, and proposed-order procedures have been followed.

A respondent's failure to answer does not eliminate the need for evidence. Party signatures, submission of a proposed decree, clerk acceptance, and the passage of time do not independently finalize the marriage.

The divorce becomes final through the court's judgment. The parties should confirm that the judge signed or rendered the final decree and should record the correct date.

LOCAL PROCEDURES

## Do statewide rules answer every county and court requirement?

No. Statewide law controls the general legal framework, but local rules and court procedures may govern:

- Case assignment
- Standing orders
- Petition attachments
- Local forms
- Financial inventories
- Parenting courses
- Parenting plans
- Mediation
- Hearing and prove-up methods
- Default packets
- Proposed-order submission
- Electronic filing details
- Artificial-intelligence certifications
- Judge-specific scheduling procedures

A court-specific instruction should not be treated as countywide unless its language supports that scope. Local requirements should be checked for the exact assigned court immediately before filing.
